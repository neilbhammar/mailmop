# Gmail Storage Full? How to Free Up Space in 2026

The "storage full" warning tends to show up at the worst time: someone is trying to send you something, or you are waiting on a password reset, and Gmail has quietly stopped accepting mail. The fix is not complicated, but most people do it in the slowest possible order (deleting recent emails one at a time) and then wonder why the number barely moves.

Here is the short version, then the full walkthrough.

**Quick answer: how to free up Gmail storage**

1. **Empty Spam and Trash.** Both count against your quota.
2. Search **`larger:10M`** and delete the big attachments you do not need. This is where the space is.
3. Search **`category:promotions older_than:1m`**, select all matching conversations, delete.
4. Find your highest volume senders and delete them in bulk (by sender, not by email).
5. **Empty Trash again.** Nothing is freed until you do.

Most inboxes recover several gigabytes in under 20 minutes with just steps 1, 2, and 5.

## What "storage full" actually means

Every free Google account gets **15GB**, shared across three products: Gmail, Google Drive, and Google Photos. If any one of them balloons, all three run out of room at the same time. So the first thing to check is which product is using the space.

Go to [one.google.com/storage](https://one.google.com/storage). You will see a bar broken down by Gmail, Drive, and Photos. If Gmail is the biggest slice, keep reading. If Photos is (very common since 2021, when new photo uploads started counting toward the quota), jump to the Drive and Photos section near the end.

When the account is full:

- You **cannot receive** new email. Senders get a bounce saying your mailbox is over quota.
- You **cannot send** email either, in most cases.
- Drive uploads and Photos backups stop.
- If the account stays over its limit for **two years**, Google's policy allows it to delete your content across Gmail, Drive, and Photos.

That last point is the reason to fix this properly rather than deleting just enough to get under the line.

## Where the space really goes

Two facts that change how you should clean up:

**Email count is not the problem. Attachments are.** A normal email without attachments is usually 20KB to 100KB. Ten thousand of them is under 1GB. Meanwhile a single video someone sent you, a PowerPoint deck, or a scanned PDF can be 10MB to 25MB (Gmail's attachment limit). A few dozen of those are worth more than every newsletter you have ever received.

**A handful of senders account for most of the volume.** In MailMop's own usage data across 16 million analyzed emails, the median inbox holds about 12,000 messages, and roughly 1 in 7 of the senders people choose to delete has more than 1,000 emails on its own. You do not have a clutter problem spread evenly across your contacts. You have a few senders that have been quietly piling up for years.

So the fastest order is: big attachments first, then bulk delete by category and sender, then everything else.

## Step 1: Empty Spam and Trash

Start here because it is free space you already decided you do not want.

- Open **Spam** in the left sidebar, click **Delete all spam messages now**.
- Open **Trash**, click **Empty Trash now**.

Both folders auto-clear after 30 days, but the stuff sitting in them right now is still counted. If you have been deleting emails for the last few days trying to fix this, Trash might be holding everything you thought you got rid of.

## Step 2: Delete the largest emails

This is the single highest impact step. Paste these into the Gmail search bar, one at a time, from the top down:

| Search | What it finds |
|---|---|
| `larger:25M` | Emails at or near Gmail's attachment limit. The worst offenders. |
| `larger:10M` | The next tier. Usually a few dozen to a few hundred results. |
| `larger:5M has:attachment` | Big attachments below the 10MB line. |
| `filename:mp4 OR filename:mov OR filename:zip` | Videos and archives regardless of size. |
| `in:sent larger:5M` | Things you sent. People forget that Sent counts too. |
| `has:attachment older_than:2y larger:2M` | Old files you have almost certainly saved elsewhere or no longer need. |

For each search:

1. Skim the list. Anything you need, open it and download the attachment first (or save it to Drive, which usually costs the same storage, so download to your computer if the goal is freeing space).
2. Click the **checkbox** at the top left of the list to select everything on the page.
3. If a banner appears saying **"Select all conversations that match this search"**, click it. Without this, Gmail only selects the 50 or 100 emails on the current page.
4. Click the **trash icon**.

You will usually find old project files, someone's vacation video, installers, and PDFs you opened once. This step alone often recovers 2GB to 5GB.

## Step 3: Bulk delete by category and age

Gmail already sorts incoming mail into categories, and those categories double as search filters. These clear thousands of low value emails in one action each:

```
category:promotions older_than:1m
category:social older_than:3m
category:updates older_than:1y
category:forums older_than:6m
```

Same routine: search, checkbox, "Select all conversations that match," delete.

Two refinements worth knowing:

- Add `-is:starred` to any search to protect anything you starred. Example: `category:promotions older_than:1m -is:starred`.
- Add `-from:` to exclude a sender you want to keep. Example: `category:updates older_than:1y -from:mybank.com`.

If you would rather go by date than category, `older_than:2y` and `before:2023/01/01` both work. Be honest about whether you will ever open those.

## Step 4: Delete by sender

Categories catch a lot, but the biggest single wins usually come from individual senders: a job board that emailed you daily for four years, a retailer you bought from once, a notification address from an app you no longer use.

To do this manually, search `from:` followed by the address, select all matching conversations, and delete. Gmail's search bar will suggest addresses as you type. The problem is knowing *which* senders to search for. Gmail gives you no list of who emails you most.

That is the part [MailMop](/) automates. It scans your inbox in your browser and returns a single list of every sender, ranked by how many emails they have sent you, with a delete button next to each. You work down the list and clear the giants. There is a fuller walkthrough in [how to delete all emails from one sender in Gmail](/blog/delete-emails-from-one-sender-gmail).

## Step 5: Empty Trash again, then verify

Everything you deleted in steps 2 through 4 is now sitting in Trash and still counted. Open **Trash** and click **Empty Trash now**.

Then check [one.google.com/storage](https://one.google.com/storage). If the number has not moved, do not panic. Google says the storage meter can take **up to 24 hours** to update after a large deletion. Check back tomorrow before deleting anything else.

## Use Google's built-in storage manager

Google also has a cleanup tool of its own at [one.google.com/storage/management](https://one.google.com/storage/management). It groups things into cards like "Emails with large attachments," "Spam emails," "Large files in Drive," and "Blurry photos."

It is a decent second pass. It will not find high volume senders, and its "large attachments" card is essentially the `larger:10M` search from step 2 with a nicer interface, but it does cover Drive and Photos in the same place, which the Gmail search bar cannot.

## If the space is in Drive or Photos

If the storage breakdown showed Drive or Photos as the main user, Gmail cleanup will not solve it.

**Google Drive**

- Open [drive.google.com/drive/quota](https://drive.google.com/drive/quota). It lists your files sorted largest first. Delete what you do not need, then empty Drive's own Trash (it has a separate one from Gmail).
- Files that other people shared with you do **not** count against your storage. Only files you own do.

**Google Photos**

- In Photos, go to **Settings**, then **Backup**, and switch upload quality from **Original** to **Storage saver**. You can also apply this to existing photos with **Recover storage**, which compresses everything already uploaded.
- The storage manager linked above has cards for screenshots, blurry photos, and large videos.

## What not to do

- **Do not archive to save space.** Archiving hides an email from your inbox but it stays in All Mail and still counts.
- **Do not delete from All Mail without a search.** You will lose things you wanted. Always search first, then select all matching.
- **Do not do a big cleanup in the phone app.** It has no "select all matching conversations" option, so you are limited to one screen of emails at a time. Use a desktop browser, or a tool that works by sender.
- **Do not skip Trash.** This is the mistake behind almost every "I deleted thousands of emails and nothing changed" complaint.

## Keep it from filling up again

A one-time cleanup buys you a year or two. These keep the number from creeping back:

**Unsubscribe from what you never read.** Deleting a newsletter today does nothing about the one arriving tomorrow. Gmail shows an Unsubscribe link next to the sender name on most marketing email, and our guide to [unsubscribing from emails in Gmail](/blog/how-to-unsubscribe-gmail-2026) covers the faster ways to do it across dozens of senders at once.

**Set a filter that deletes on arrival.** Settings, then **Filters and Blocked Addresses**, then **Create a new filter**. Put a sender or a term like `unsubscribe` in the criteria, choose **Delete it** as the action. Anything matching skips your inbox and your storage.

**Repeat one search a month.** `category:promotions older_than:1m` once a month, select all, delete, empty Trash. It takes about a minute.

**Save attachments outside Google.** If a file matters, download it to your computer or an external drive and delete the email. Moving it to Drive just shifts the same bytes to a different slice of the same 15GB.

## Should you just pay for more storage?

Sometimes, yes. Google One is **$1.99 a month for 100GB** in the US, which is cheap if the alternative is an hour of cleanup every year. It makes sense if you genuinely need to keep everything (business records, legal, an archive you search often), or if Photos is the real consumer and you do not want to compress anything.

It makes less sense if the 15GB is being eaten by promotional email and attachments you will never open again. Paying to store junk is still storing junk, and the inbox itself stays just as cluttered.
